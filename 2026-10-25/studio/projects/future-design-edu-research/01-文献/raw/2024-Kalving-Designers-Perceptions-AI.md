The creative industries are at a significant turning point, blurring the lines between human innovation and machine efficiency due to the advent of AI tools for design. This research addresses the design community's reaction to generative AI's rapid ascent. Involving both professionals and students, this paper uses a survey (n=49), focus groups (n=6), and interviews (n=6), to explore the perspectives on AI's role in creative workflows, revealing a mix of enthusiasm and dread for its potential to enhance innovation and concerns about its fit in traditionally human realms. The prevailing view frames AI as a partner or co-worker in design, yet calls for ethical considerations and transparent practices in its applications. This exploration aims to understand current designer attitudes and presents an initial framework for the adoption of AI tools in the designer's workflow.

1 INTRODUCTION

The current era, already referred to as the “Age of AI”, [31] has witnessed the rise of innovation processes and tools that integrate Artificial Intelligence (AI). This has led to unprecedented opportunities and challenges [28]. The initial application of business algorithms, such as those utilized by Alibaba, has now expanded into the realm of creativity, as evidenced by the Milan Design Week where a 3D-printed chair designed using Artificial Intelligence (AI) was showcased [24]. The conversation surrounding AI has moved swiftly from academic journals and technology industry boardrooms to the public arena. This shift is largely attributed to the rapid growth of Generative AI [9] and with the advancement in large language models (LLM), AI was brought into common knowledge [32]. There has been significant progress in AI technology, particularly with the advent of generative AI (GenAI) models like Stable Diffusion (2022), Midjourney (2022), and DALL-E (2021). OpenAI's development of ChatGPT in 2023 was a notable milestone, as it has distinguished itself through its advanced reasoning, comprehension, and interactive abilities [33]. As these technologies have become more integrated into society, the public's interest, curiosity, and concerns have surfaced. These AI enabled the creation of artwork, music, and design elements that were previously inconceivable without human intervention.

According to a 2023 ETLA Economic Research study [17], one in five Finnish workers have jobs where at least half of their tasks could be automated, and two-thirds can use AI for 20% of their tasks. Finland tops the EU's Digital Economy and Society Index (DESI), tracking digital skills, connectivity, technology integration, and public services [8]. Finland's digital readiness makes it an ideal country for studying AI's impact on design, as Nordic industrial companies are increasingly committed to AI, investing heavily in its development [1].

Our research focuses on designers’ use of AI tools, involving professionals and students in Finland. To understand how these tools should be further developed, including ethical considerations, it is necessary to first understand how they are currently used. However, there is a research gap in our understanding of how designers view and incorporate AI-driven image and text creation tools into their work processes.

This research captures a fleeting moment in a rapidly evolving field, as we stand at the crossroads of how AI and design practices will integrate. Its primary objective is to explore designers’ perceptions of AI, focusing on their utilization and acceptance of AI tools within the design process. The research involves both professionals and students to investigate how designers in Finland currently employ AI tools in their design work, discern the factors influencing the perceived acceptability of AI among designers, and evaluate the perceived benefits and challenges of using AI in design projects. By incorporating perspectives from students through surveys and focus groups, whose identities and professional outlooks are still forming, the study gains valuable information that highlights the evolving nature of AI's role. Professionals contribute to the interviews with a layer of knowledge about the workflows of experienced designers, while students, although lacking extensive knowledge and understanding, provide fresh insights and perspectives.

Such insights can aid in developing more user-friendly AI tools that cater to designers’ needs. Based on the collected insights, we propose an initial framework for AI tool usage by designers, outlining the value of AI, human aspects, and the new demands placed on designers.

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Evolution of Design

The word “design” can be used as both a noun, to indicate the final creation, and a verb, to denote the act of creating[30]. The term “designer” has evolved from solely a noun that identifies someone who designs to also being used as an adjective to describe items, typically suggesting quality and exclusivity [19].

The field of Industrial Design has evolved with technology, leading to new roles like interaction designers (UI) and user experience (UX) professionals. The rise of smart devices and the Internet of Things (IoT) has created products that blend digital and physical worlds, pushing UI and UX designers to address design issues beyond screens and into the physical realm[18].

The design process itself is a problem-solving method that involves defining problems, devising solutions, and implementing them. By employing design methods designers can employ these methods to detect and solve issues early on, leading to more effective solutions and improved design outcomes [5]. Users commonly employ the term “design” to refer to a final product. However, it is ineffective to categorize design solely based on its end product, because the solution emerges through the design process and often does not exist before it [2]. Dubberly (7) cataloged over a hundred design and development processes across various fields. These methodologies range from mnemonic devices such as the 4Ds (define, design, develop, deploy) to complex models like Archer's 9-phase, 229-step systematic method.

Design has progressed from focusing solely on the creation of visually appealing objects to the creation of unique human experiences [3]. This evolution has placed a greater emphasis on sustainability, user-centered design, and social impact, highlighting an expansion in the field's ethical and practical considerations [18].

2.2 Tracing the Evolution of AI in Creative Practices

AI has been described as a system's capacity to accurately interpret external data, learn from it, and apply that knowledge to achieve specific goals and tasks through flexible adaptation [13]. This broad area encompasses several subfields, such as machine learning, computer vision, and deep learning.

Designers have been using computer-aided design (CAD), digital typography, and user interfaces to create designs for several decades. It is debatable whether AI art has been around as long as computers or digital art [22]. Design, central to innovation, involves generating ideas and solving problems, historically driven by human decision-making [28]. However, Verganti et al. 28 identified that algorithms are playing an increasing role in creative problem-solving, shifting human design focus to sensemaking, i.e. deciding which problems to tackle. This evolution necessitates further research and new theoretical frameworks.

The term “computational creativity”, referring to AI systems capable of creativity, dates back to at least the 1970s[16]. By automating repetitive tasks and generating unique and unexpected content, these systems are evolving from passive tools to active, autonomous collaborators. They work alongside human creators, transforming the creative process into a co-creative partnership [11].

Muller et al. (23) notes that emerging GenAI methods have the potential to produce creative outputs that are unique, valuable, and unexpected in a given context. This means that AI can facilitate creativity in various forms, such as images, text, music, video, code, and other forms of design. The collaboration between humans and AI varies across domains, typically humans lead creative, strategic, and compassionate tasks while AI leads routine optimization tasks [34]. Li et al. (21) found that experienced designers trust their originality, creativity, and empathy, and see GenAI as a helpful tool. They emphasized that humans should control AI alignment, valuing “enjoyment” and “agency” in the creative process. Vimpari et al. (29) noted the need for rich, reliable, and transparent insights into how professionals perceive, adopt, and use creative AI in different industries. They found that the game industry already widely uses text-to-image prompting technology, which rivals the work of professional creatives and sparks discussions about the future of creative work, job displacement, and copyright issues.

Recent work has focused on developing interfaces and frameworks that enable co-creation between users and AI [25] [12] [20]. However, at the end of 2022 and the beginning of 2023, OpenAI's ChatGPT and similar services made GenAI accessible to everyone through user-friendly interfaces. Utilizing large language models and machine learning, GenAI has significantly changed the field. Despite these advancements, many recorded use cases and studies are still treating AI as tools rather than as collaborative partners [11].

To chart designers’ perceptions and practices with AI-enhanced tools for design, we utilized a mixed methods approach, combining qualitative and quantitative research methods [27]. This approach helps obtain a better understanding of the research problem and capture the complexity of the human phenomena by combining the strengths of both methods [4, 6]. We applied an online survey (n = 49), focus groups (n = 6), and interviews (n = 6) as research methods, with bachelor and master-level design students participating in the survey and focus groups, and professionals participating in the interviews.

This comprehensive data collection effort illustrated in figure 1, offers a rich, multifaceted view of the intersections between art, design, and technology from the perspectives of creatives and professionals in the field.

3.1 Survey

The survey was completed in February 2023 by 49 participants, revealing a diverse group predominantly composed of young adults with a median age of 23 years (38 women, 3 men, 4 non-binary, 4 N/A). The survey targeted second-year university design students in Finland, chosen for their potential to offer fresh insights into the future of design. The survey assessed participants’ background, technical skills, and awareness of AI, requiring explanations of AI in their own words before and after presenting an official definition. It included rating scale and open-text questions on AI ethics, use, trust, and future outlooks. The online survey, averaging 14.6 minutes to complete, was conducted anonymously using the Webropol tool. The data was processed and analyzed in Excel for both quantitative and qualitative insights, revealing user attitudes, preferences, and issues relevant to the study's objectives.

3.2 Focus Groups

After conducting a survey, two focus group sessions were organized in April 2023, attended by six (3+3) bachelor or postgraduate level students participants, all women, averaging a median age of 23.6 years. The focus group discussed deeper into specific AI use cases and situations needing clarification. Participants, recruited from the university, gave informed consent and shared their AI experiences via a questionnaire. The agenda for the focus group sessions was as follows:

Consent form and background questionnaire

Prompt: ChatGPT

Prompt: Self-Driving Bus

Prompt: Medical AI Diagnosis

Exercise: Mapping Use-Cases

Exercise: Considerations for Developing AI


Facilitated by a researcher, the discussions spanned topics like ChatGPT, self-driving buses, and medical AI diagnosis, alongside exercises mapping use cases and considerations for AI development. Participants reflected on their initial and current AI tools usage, discussing preferences and reasons behind them. Discussions, initiated through newspaper articles, culminated in participants noting their AI-related thoughts for home, work, and medical uses on Post-it notes, and summarizing key points for AI developers to consider.

For analysis, the focus group sessions were recorded, transcribed, and anonymized. The anonymized transcripts were then examined using Atlas.ti software, coding text segments to identify themes. This thematic organization allowed for pattern recognition and insight into collective perceptions, experiences, and opinions, offering a rich qualitative dimension to the research alongside surveys and interviews.

3.3 Interviews
Figure 2: The first interview analysis board visualized all the main data findings to identify patterns and connections between the use cases, focusing on where and when AI was used and why. The results were documented digitally. This was followed by the creation of a second board for further analysis, and finally, a third board, where the framework was mapped out.

Six (6) interviews were conducted in December 2023 and January 2024 in Finland to gain a deeper understanding of the subject matter. The interviews featured a balanced group of participants, with two (2) identifying as women and four (4) as men. The median age of the interviewees was 29.5 years, with ages ranging from 23 to 57 years old. Each interviewee had completed their education in design or was employed in roles related to their field, such as product designers, design researchers, and 3D visualizers. The insights gathered from these professionals provided valuable information as they actively applied their creative skills in the workforce.

The open-ended interviews were utilized to explore the intricate experiences, methods, and processes of designers working with AI.

These nine questions asked participants to describe their role and experience with design tools, including Visual AI; the frequency of AI tool integration in their design process; specific projects where AI was beneficial; factors influencing their perception of AI in design; their views on the acceptability of AI tools within the design community; any formal training received on AI tools; effective resources for staying updated on AI technologies; desired improvements in AI tools; and their perspectives on future trends in AI integration.

This approach allowed participants to offer detailed insights into their use of AI tools, the challenges and opportunities they face, their views on AI's acceptability in the design process, and their suggestions for improvements. The interviews, lasting 20-30 minutes, were conducted both in-person and via Microsoft Teams, with each session recorded transcribed, and anonymized with consent, ensuring anonymity and transparency.

The analysis combined Cognitive Mapping and Affinity Diagramming to process the data. Cognitive Mapping helped visualize complex mental models[15], while Affinity Diagramming organized ideas into clusters based on similarities, facilitating a structured overview of themes[14]. This innovative analytical approach allowed for a deeper understanding of the data, revealing nuanced insights into designers’ experiences and perspectives on AI. The practical application involved visually arranging interview data on boards of varying sizes to refine themes and connections, leading to well-informed research findings, fig. 2.

4 RESULTS

The research results report on the perceived benefits of AI, including creative content generation, efficiency, and task automation. They reveal ethical dilemmas and practical challenges associated with integrating AI into creative processes and provide insights into the emerging perception of AI as a collaborative force within the design ecosystem.

4.1 Survey Insights
Figure 3: Survey respondents’ ratings (n=49).

Survey respondents’ (R) rating scale answers are summarised in figure 3, which sums up how participants rated their use, trust, and ethical concerns regarding AI tools. Altogether, respondents’ use of AI tools varied broadly, with some participants reporting regular use of AI tools, while others reported no use.

Of the 49 participants, 43 respondents considered that they knew what AI was, and the remainder were less sure. Provided definitions of AI ranged from metaphorical expressions such as, “Good servant, bad master” (R9) to more technical explanations, e.g., “...machine learning... identifies patterns and detects errors” (R8). Most participants primarily referred to text or image creation in their descriptions, i.e. generative AI.

One-quarter of survey respondents (12/49) mentioned that they lacked awareness of when they were using AI and an understanding of how to utilize AI tools. As one respondent expressed, “I probably am not even aware how much I use them” (R27). Almost all participants recognized ethical issues surrounding using AI tools. When asked about the ethical issues surrounding AI, copyright and security issues were noted by over half of the participants (26/49) mentioning concerns such as art theft, social media data extraction, and audio surveillance.

Participants reported trust in AI tools was quite broad-ranging. Four participants (4/49) stated that they did not trust AI's outputs, one writing, “The problem at the moment is that the chatbots will confidently lie to you”(R3). Four respondents (4/49) noted that the use case affected the trust in an AI-integrated tool, service, or product, e.g., “I wouldn't trust AI on medical issues or anything related. I would trust and use AI in photo editing”(R19). User experience, transparency, and the knowledge of the developer were each mentioned by four (4/49) of respondents as factors contributing to trust. Contrary to these themes, two survey respondents (2/49)mentioned they would trust AI more than humans, stating, “People always want something out of you and AI cannot yet deceive you”(R10).

The majority of participants reported a desire to increase their usage of AI tools in the future. However, altogether (7) of the respondents expressed that they had no interest or need for AI tools. This was explained to be due to their lack of intuitiveness, training, or bad experiences, e.g., “I worked on my [job application] cover letter with Bing Chat, and it gave some ideas, but most of them were terrible,... and I figured I'll just do it myself” (R3). When asked how they saw themselves using AI tools, in the future, respondents replied with various use cases, including learning purposes (2/49), ideation (3/49), food recommendations (1/49), research tools (1/49), assistive technologies for the elderly (1/49), or integrated with robotics (3/49).

4.2 Focus Group Discoveries

The focus groups were conducted in two sessions, with the first session involving three participants (P1-3) and the second session also involving three participants (P4-6). Similarly, as in the survey, focus group participants’ experiences with AI varied significantly. Two-thirds mentioned using AI for creating visuals, with tools like DALL·E, Midjourney, and Diffusion Bee. Again, there was also confusion about whether AI had actually been used, its use, as highlighted by participant 1 (P1): “I used this text-to-speech tool, and I didn't know if it was AI-generated or something.” Understanding of AI ranged from seeing it as a tool for generating content to a system capable of autonomous decision-making.

The role of AI in creativity stirred mixed feelings, with the participants feeling ethically conflicted about using AI for design. For academic tasks, AI was positively perceived, with P2 stating, “Currently, I'm regularly using ChatGPT because I need to read a lot of references of academic articles,” while skepticism about AI's creative capability was evident. For instance, P4 placed critique after testing DALL·E: “We asked DALL·E to create a similar jewelry stand for us... and then what it suggested that well, I can do better as an industrial designer than this.” While participants were interested in text-based AI tools for their practical applications, they were apprehensive about AI's role in creative fields and its potential to replace human jobs.

When different scenarios were discussed, participants preferred human intervention to ensure safety and empathy in automated services. Regarding automated buses, participants expressed a preference for human oversight, with P3 sharing, “I will feel safer because it [the automated bus] follows their own road and nothing can come in front of it,” suggesting trust in automation with caveats. The trust in AI's analysis of personal data was tentative, with a preference for human validation in medical decisions. P4’s concern, “If the machine said there's no problem and I feel like I'm going to die, then it would be nice if I could still see a doctor”, illustrates the nuanced trust in AI's accuracy.

AI's integration into daily life was perceived to balance between the excitement for innovation with critical reflections on its implications for work, creativity, and societal well-being. Altogether, focus group participants believed that greater transparency, ethical considerations, and human oversight were necessary for AI development.

4.3 Interview results

To further explore the themes that emerged from the survey and focus groups, a set of interviews was conducted with professional designers. Two interviews were conducted online on Microsoft teams, while four were conducted face-to-face.

The interviewees, i.e. designers already graduated and in working life, reported active use of AI tools. Most participants (5/6) regularly used text-based AI tools like ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, and Bing, and similarly, image creation tools such as DALL-E, alongside visual editing software. A third (2/6) also utilized AI-enhanced layout/design tools like Canva. It was typical to self-learn the tools via various online platforms, as expressed e.g. by participant 6 (P6): “I usually pick up a tool from Instagram, from another designer and try it out for fun; if I like it, I'll use it more.” P1 pointed out that s/he had observed the AI tools’ transition from a novelty to something with practical utility.

The primary motivation for AI tool usage was to alleviate manual cognitive tasks. P6 noted a preference for tools that “reduce annoying tasks”, while P3 viewed AI as a “computer brain that thinks for you”. Participants increasingly integrated AI into their workflows for inspiration and enhancing creativity. P2 mentioned using ChatGPT alongside Google for benchmarking, while P1 appreciated AI's unique ideas. Some used AI visuals as placeholders in design drafts, with P3 utilizing an AI generator for early poster ideas and P6 occasionally incorporating AI-generated content into professional work. P4 acknowledged using AI for time-saving, albeit with reservations about the quality. AI's role in collaborative settings when working on a design was also discussed, with P5 noting its utility in idea-generation phases, effectively replacing the need for human discussion in iterating ideas. P3 highlighted AI's efficiency and objectivity, finding it faster and less biased than discussing with a (work) supervisor.

Ethical concerns were raised by 4/6 participants about the supervision and authenticity of AI-generated content. The elderly's vulnerability to being deceived by AI images was a particular concern, with P5 noting the potential for negative actions based on such misinformation. P4 criticized the ethical implications of AI in advertising, using Hesburger's AI-generated Christmas ads as an example of “laziness”. This sentiment echoed a broader desire for maintaining human creativity and ethical guidelines in AI use, with P6 advocating for technology's alignment with the common good. Despite the ethical dilemmas, participants recognized AI's potential to automate mundane tasks and boost creativity.

The future role of designers was debated, with current technology limitations noted as limiting “I can do better, I just use it to save time, but most of the tools don't produce yet good enough results for regular use” (P4) and “It can't do what a human can; a designer will always be needed” (P1). It became clear that designers are now balancing the embrace of technology and its advances when dealing with complex problems, and the pressure to maintain human creativity and oversight to the work itself, as well as the ethical considerations. Participants also recognized strongly the change and that the role of designers was evolving, with a shift towards supervisory and facilitating roles over AI and the design process as a whole. “The immediate output that you get out of these tools is aesthetically very nice. So, designers’ [traditional] value has kind of disappeared” (P2). With a similar theme another participant said, “I think we will need to focus more on soft skills, like empathy, interaction and listening to everyone, which are crucial when dealing with multiple stakeholders. But not just from the perspective of what is expected of us. We will need to take on a facilitator role, acting as a moderator. But will it be designers who get hired for this role? I don't know, but I believe it's important that we channel our expertise here. I do believe that traditional UI design will disappear. Those who continue to do it will need to be really good. Unfortunately, we may see a lot of visual workplaces disappear, which is a real pity” (P6). This evolution points to a future where manual labor is minimized, and designers play a crucial role in guiding the ethical and creative use of AI in their field. Designers realized that they needed to leverage their skill set of softer skills, such as empathy.

5 DISCUSSION
5.1 The Role of AI Tools for Designers

The research results discuss the perceived benefits of AI, including creative content generation, efficiency, and task automation. Our research suggests, that designers believe AI can automate some tasks, but not replicate human understanding. To survive designers must yet again adapt, focusing on empathy, and guiding AI use ethically. By embracing AI as a collaborator, designers can enhance the design process. Throughout the interviews, it became clear professional designers favor intuitive tools. As pointed out by Shi et al. (26), we should strive to design AI for the user, instead of making the user adapt to the AI. The student participants in the focus groups felt uncertain about AI's benefits beyond basic tasks, indicating the importance of recognizing the practical benefits, a core element in earlier acceptance models [10].

Initial Framework. Our studies, especially the interview answers, revealed that professional designers heavily rely on community recommendations when choosing tools. They thoroughly test these tools before incorporating them into their workflows, iterating and re-evaluating based on the results. Throughout the evaluation stage in iterative design, designers measure the tool to analyze technical, practical, and ethical aspects. Positive experiences are then shared through various channels like casual conversations and social media. Building on this, we propose a model as an initial framework to outline how designers adopt and utilize new AI tools and technologies. This model consists of four key stages: community Recommendation, Evaluation of the tool, Iteration for iterative design cycles and refining the tool usage, and the Release of the tool and experiences, Figure 4. Successful tools and recommendations for them are often shared within the community. On the other hand, the use of tools that fail to meet expectations is discontinued, and another tool is tried out, typically again based on the recommendation by the community.

Figure 4: The process involves gathering recommendations from the community, evaluating technical and ethical aspects, engaging in iterative development, and sharing the product with the community if it meets the required standards. This iterative loop continues with another design task or AI tool if the initial steps of the loop are not successfully completed.

These four stages are illustrated in the framework Figure 4, beginning with community recommendations, which describes how participants often discover new design tools through recommendations from their community or networks. For example, one interview participant noted, “I was suggested to use ChatGPT to ask it for a starting point or some outlines on something, and then I was inspired by that to start somewhere” (P3). Other participants stated, “I follow designers on Instagram, and usually, someone has used these [AI tools], and then I try them out” (P1), and “I have this Daily News feed... it regularly suggests new AI tools or new research on AI that I might be interested in” (P2).

AI tools typically undergo a user evaluation period, a trial and testing phase, where they are assessed to determine if they satisfy users’ ethical, technical, and visual requirements before being adopted for regular use. One survey participant noted, “Out of curiosity, I've tried AI tools, but I haven't found them useful enough for myself, so I don't use them” (R29). Another survey participant mentioned specific challenges “I could see myself using an AI assistant like Bing Chat if only it were better” (R1).

The third phase of the framework, iteration, was used by designers as they refined their work through multiple evaluation cycles. For example, one participant shared, “I used the AI generator to create multiple rounds of images based on the description of the concept...” (P3). Another mentioned, “It probably took quite a long time to get the kind of output I was expecting... but I think the results were better than if I had pasted these kinds of icon-based images together myself” (P2). However, the current iteration methods were found lacking, as one participant noted, “The process when you put a request into AI to get a visual output and then you want to iterate it, the iteration doesn't happen exactly as you want it to be, and I think this is because of a mismatch between the way people think and the way AI is thinking” (P2).

In the framework's release phase, users actively share AI tools they have found particularly effective. As one participant noted, “When something really works, we always talk about it at lunch” (P3). Others recognized that simply using AI tools was a way of contributing: “By using it, I am contributing to its development and use” (R48). This feedback then loops into the recommendation phase of the framework, allowing each new project and use case to be shaped by these voluntary and involuntary shared experiences.

5.2 AI as a Collaborative Partner

The discussions in the conducted research indicated a preference for a “human in the loop” approach to AI integration. In focus group scenarios, participants expressed a desire for human influence, such as emergency stop buttons on autonomous vehicles, highlighting the importance of maintaining human control over AI systems. Furthermore, AI was increasingly seen either as a soundboard to bounce ideas at, as well as a collaborative partner in the design process. The participants in the interviews mentioned that AI could make the designer's job easier, with the designer playing a facilitating role. This perception of AI could significantly change designers’ roles, as they would act as facilitators, curators, and integrators of AI-generated content, focusing on a comprehensive narrative. However, it was noted that integrating AI into design did not mean diminishing the designer's identity but rather expanding it. Designers’ inherent skills, such as understanding users and empathy, were seen to play a crucial role in integrating AI into the design process and in the ethical AI use in design. Indeed, in Zhu et al.’s Spectrum of Initiative, the most advanced level positions the AI as a collaborator, actively participating in the design process alongside human designers [35].

5.3 Ethical and Practical Concerns

The ethical dilemmas with AI tools were widely recognized by designers. Ethical concerns included copyright issues and the legitimacy of results, which emerged as important themes in each part of our research. The integration of AI into daily life has been subtle yet profound, evident in services like Google search rankings, Siri, and image recognition. Especially the survey responses highlighted the concern that people were not certain when they had been using AI. It is likely that the most significant effects of AI on individuals arise from these inconspicuous AI applications, which may introduce biases or influence user behavior.

Also, practical challenges associated with integrating AI into creative processes emerged from the findings. Although the interview participants appreciated the usability and the seamlessness of the AI tools, the discussions suggested that those tools may potentially obscure the presence of AI, and create doubts about the trustworthiness of online systems. Moreover, using AI tools could be seen as laziness from the designer's side. Also, the quality of the output in AI-generated content was not always satisfactory for the designers.

5.4 Future Directions and Limitations of the Research

The research underlines AI's transformative potential in creative industries while advocating for a balanced approach that addresses both ethical and practical considerations. It emphasizes the importance of maintaining designers’ central role in guiding AI integration into their work. As design practices evolve, fostering a cooperative relationship between human creativity and machine intelligence will be crucial for pushing the boundaries of creative innovation. This exploratory study prompts additional inquiries. Further investigation is needed to explore how AI assistance and tools can be effectively integrated into different design methodologies.

While addressing methodological issues, this research acknowledges its limitations. In particular, involving students, that may have limited work experience and developing design thinking skills may result in more basic views on AI in design. However, they also bring perspectives as individuals forming their design identities and developing their own workflows, which adds an interesting dimension to the research.

Additionally, differences in research methods between the two groups, professionals and students, such as the insights gathered from surveys versus interviews, also influence the difference in the depth of the results. Despite this, presenting perspectives from both students and professionals enhances the overall understanding of various use cases and attitudes toward AI tools.

Although the study utilized a modest sample size, the combination of different research methods may have helped mitigate this limitation to some extent. Confining the participant pool to Finland may limit the generalizability of findings to countries, but the insights gained have the potential to be applicable across different regions. Further research in diverse contexts is needed to enrich the research topic.

6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have reported designers’ perceptions of AI, collected through an online survey (n=49), focus groups (n=6), and interviews (n=6), involving both professionals and students, at a time when GenAI tools were rapidly emerging. The research revealed that designers see several advantages and opportunities in using AI, such as its potential for creative content creation, improved efficiency, and automation of mundane tasks. AI was seen as a means to expand creative possibilities and support innovative thinking. While the benefits of AI were clearly recognized, participants expressed concerns about integrating AI into deeply human domains, such as art. Ethical considerations, like copyright issues and the legitimacy of results, emerged as important themes in each part of the study, highlighting both philosophical aspects and practical frustrations, such as a potentially AI-generated advertisement.

Furthermore, the study revealed that AI is increasingly seen as a collaborative partner in the design process. This perception of AI could significantly change designers’ roles, as they would act as facilitators, curators, and integrators of AI-generated content, focusing on a comprehensive narrative. However, it was mostly seen that integrating AI into design does not diminish the designer's identity but rather expands it. Designers’ inherent skills, such as understanding and empathy towards users, play a crucial role in integrating AI into the design process and in ethical AI use and design. Designers recognized that they needed to adapt and expand their skills, focusing on empathy, cultural understanding, and interpretation of human emotions and societal trends. By embracing AI as a collaborator, designers can redefine their identity and shape a future where human creativity and AI coexist to enhance the design process.
